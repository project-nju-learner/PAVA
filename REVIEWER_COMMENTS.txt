Paper Summary:
The paper proposes VideoPursuit, an agentic framework for long-video question answering. Its central idea is to treat retrieval for a local information need as a persistent local process, rather than returning control to global reasoning after every retrieval action. A controller identifies an information gap and delegates a neutral retrieval objective to a retrieval agent. The local agent can perform several acquisitions around that objective, while a local audit decides whether to deliver the observations, repair the retrieval, or report a limitation. Only selected observations and source information are returned to global reasoning. Using Qwen3.8-27B, offline WhisperX transcripts, an initial 128-frame overview, and an OpenCLIP temporal index, the method is evaluated on LVBench, LongVideoBench, Video-MME Long, and MLVU. It reports an average accuracy of 82.0%. On a 597-question ablation subset, persistent local retrieval achieves 81.1%, compared with 68.7% when local status is reported but control returns after every action. Restricting information propagation across the local-global boundary also outperforms exposing either the full global context locally or the full local trace globally.
Presentation: 3: Clear and easy to follow.
Informativeness: 3: I gained a substantially deeper understanding of the problem or question.
Soundness: 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper’s soundness.
Critical Strengths:
1. The paper isolates a clear and practically relevant agent-design question. The comparison between immediate action return, return with local-status feedback, and persistent local execution directly tests whether a local objective should retain control across multiple acquisitions. The 12.4-point difference between status feedback and persistent retrieval is substantial and cannot be explained by a larger acquisition count, since the full method actually uses fewer acquisition actions on average.
2. The local-global information interface is carefully designed and documented. The controller, retrieval agent, and audit have distinct responsibilities, and the appendix specifies their inputs, outputs, stopping rules, action ceilings, and prompts. The information-boundary ablations and qualitative examples support the claim that selectively returning observations can prevent provisional local interpretations from contaminating global reasoning.
Critical Weaknesses:
1. The headline comparison mixes substantially different models, inputs, and evaluation pipelines. Published baselines use different reasoning backbones, visual models, transcript availability, frame budgets, and agent-call budgets. The same-backbone direct Qwen baseline is valuable, but it does not distinguish the VideoPursuit control structure from a strong alternative agent using the same backbone, tools, prompts, and total inference budget. The 3.4-point claimed improvement over the leading published agent therefore should not be interpreted as a clean method-level comparison.
2. The ablations establish that the authors’ implementation of persistent local retrieval substantially outperforms action-level return, but they provide limited evidence about the mechanism behind this unusually large gain. Persistent execution simultaneously changes state continuity, objective stability, context exposure, prompt structure, and the locus of control. The paper does not disentangle these factors or quantify how often later local acquisitions repair a specific gap identified by the audit. As a result, the experiments demonstrate an implementation-level effect but do not yet establish the proposed process-level explanation.
Questions For Authors:
1. Can the authors report per-benchmark ablations, paired per-question outcome changes, confidence intervals or paired significance tests, and the random seed/question roster for the 597-question subset? Are the conclusions stable across multiple subsets or decoding runs?
2. Can the authors disentangle why persistent local retrieval improves accuracy by 12.4 points over local-status feedback? In particular, please separately control for preservation of the local trajectory, reuse of the exact same objective, context length, prompt/token budget, and the location of the continuation decision. It would also be valuable to report how often a second or later local acquisition resolves the gap identified by the preceding audit, changes the final answer, or causes harmful local fixation.

Paper Summary:
Summary This paper studies the granularity of retrieval control in agentic long-video understanding: existing methods return control to global reasoning after each retrieval action, but a single action often only narrows a range and leaves the local objective unresolved. VideoPursuit organizes retrieval for each local objective as a persistent process, where a retrieval agent runs multiple actions within a bounded scope, uses a local audit to decide when to hand off, selectively returns only observations, and separates local retrieval adequacy from global answer sufficiency. With a Qwen3.8-27B backbone it reaches 82.0% average across four benchmarks. Contributions 1. It identifies a reasonable control gap: judging retrieval against the original question makes it hard to tell whether the local objective is resolved. 2. It proposes VideoPursuit, delegating each local objective to a dedicated agent that retrieves persistently, decides handoff locally, and returns only observations, positioned as a framework-level contribution for long-video retrieval. 3. It validates on four benchmarks and ablates persistent local retrieval and the information boundary. Validation The method averages 82.0% across four benchmarks, 10.4 points over same-backbone direct inference and 3.4 over AVP. Ablations on 597 questions show persistent local retrieval (81.1%) clearly beats Action Return (64.0%) and Terminate Status (68.7%) while using fewer actions, and the selective information boundary outperforms full two-way sharing, which is fairly convincing. The main limitation is that the primary comparison is cross-backbone and system-level, so mechanism attribution is not clean, and there is no fair comparison against the latest native long-context models.
Presentation: 2: Generally understandable, but requires substantial effort in places.
Informativeness: 2: I learned something interesting or useful.
Soundness: 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper’s soundness.
Critical Strengths:
1. The core insight is well-motivated: it explicitly separates local retrieval adequacy from global answer sufficiency, a sound design principle for long-video tasks that must first locate an event before comparing relations.
2. The central ablation is fairly convincing: persistent local retrieval (81.1%) clearly beats Action Return (64.0%) and Terminate Status (68.7%) while using fewer retrieval actions, so the gain is not from simply retrieving more.
3. Implementation and reproducibility material is reasonably complete: the appendix details the temporal index construction, retrieval action types, budgets, and each agent's inputs/outputs, with two timestamped case trajectories.
Critical Weaknesses:
1. The main comparison is cross-system, so gains cannot be attributed to the mechanism. VideoPursuit uses Qwen3.8-27B while the strongest baseline AVP uses Gemini-2.5-Pro, with different backbones, tools, and budgets; Direct Inference is not a fair agentic baseline either. A ReAct/AVP-style loop under the same backbone, tools, and budget is needed.
2. The core ablation does not isolate individual mechanisms. The 12.4-point gain over Terminate Status mixes objective holding, local-state preservation, interrupting global reasoning, and context isolation, and the information-boundary drops are uncontrolled for context length and evidence, which the paper itself concedes. Controlled variants are missing.
3. End-to-end cost is absent and reproduction is hard. The 204 frames exclude OpenCLIP indexing, Controller calls, tokens, and latency; the backbone is cited as Qwen3.8-Max but called Qwen3.8-27B, and prompts are abridged. Report calls, tokens, latency, and indexing cost, and recheck the 81.1% under matched budget.
Questions For Authors:
1. Under the same Qwen3.8-27B backbone, retrieval tools, and compute budget, if an AVP/ReAct-style global retrieval loop is reimplemented and compared head-to-head, does VideoPursuit still outperform it? Can the current 3.4-point cross-system gap be read as mechanism-level causal evidence?
2. Can you add a controlled variant that still returns to global reasoning each step but forces the same fixed local objective, to separate the benefit of persistent local retrieval from objective holding? What are the relative contributions within the 12.4-point gain?
3. In the information-boundary study, when observations and token counts are exactly matched and only the exposure of the Local Audit conclusion is changed, do the 4.0/5.4-point gains persist? How do you rule out confounds from context length and evidence differences, given the paper concedes the two runs differ in their sampled observations?
4. Can you report end-to-end cost: average calls per question, each agent's input/output tokens, total latency, and the OpenCLIP indexing overhead and its reuse count? Does the 81.1% ablation advantage hold under matched compute budget? Also, please clarify whether the backbone is Qwen3.8-27B or the Qwen3.8-Max in the citation.
5. As frontier models rapidly improve, both closed (e.g., 6astra) and open (e.g., Qwen3.8-27B) models offer ever stronger native long-context video understanding. Is the video reasoning task this paper targets still a real problem? More importantly, relative to simply plugging such strong models into a general-purpose base harness (e.g., Codex), where does your method's value and irreplaceability lie? Please provide a direct comparison against a "base harness + frontier model" setup.

Paper Summary:
This paper introduces VideoPursuit, a hierarchical retrieval framework for long-video question answering. It addresses the problem that returning to global reasoning after every retrieval action can interrupt ongoing local exploration. The retrieval agent instead continues gathering information toward a fixed local objective, using a local audit to decide whether further retrieval is needed. It then returns observations and their sources to global reasoning while excluding local plans and tentative conclusions. The framework combines video frames, speech transcripts, and OpenCLIP-based candidate segment navigation to gather evidence and generate answers within a shared action budget. Using Qwen3.8-27B, it achieves an average accuracy of 82.0% across four long-video QA benchmarks. Ablation results also support the effectiveness of persistent local retrieval and the separation of local and global information.
Presentation: 3: Clear and easy to follow.
Informativeness: 2: I learned something interesting or useful.
Soundness: 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper’s soundness.
Critical Strengths:
1.The paper addresses a concrete and practically relevant problem: a single retrieval action often provides a useful lead without resolving the information gap that prompted it, and returning immediately to global reasoning can interrupt further exploration. The framework keeps retrieval focused on the same local objective, uses local audits to determine whether more information is needed, and controls the handoff to prevent local plans and tentative conclusions from entering global reasoning. The design is clear, and its components work toward a common goal.

2.The paper evaluates the method on LVBench, LongVideoBench, Video-MME Long, and MLVU, achieving an average accuracy of 82.0%. This is 10.4 percentage points higher than direct inference with the same backbone and also exceeds the average results reported for AVP and Symphony in the table. The ablations show that persistent local retrieval outperforms returning to global reasoning after every action. They also show that, with persistent local retrieval retained, additionally exposing the full global context or returning the local trajectory reduces accuracy. These results provide reasonably convincing support for the core design choices.
Critical Weaknesses:
1.The distinction from prior work needs to be made clearer. VideoExplorer, REVEAL, and VideoSEAL have already explored hierarchical retrieval, evidence verification, and the separation of planning from answering. This paper places further emphasis on persistent local retrieval and controlled information handoff, but its ablations mainly compare workflow variants constructed by the authors. These comparisons do not yet establish what capabilities the proposed design adds beyond existing frameworks. The main-table comparisons also involve different backbones and input settings, so a direct comparison supporting the claimed methodological distinction is still missing.

2.The full inference cost has not been adequately evaluated. The appendix reports that the full method uses an average of 3.7 acquisition actions, compared with 4.6 for Action-Level Return, showing that its gains do not come from taking more retrieval actions. However, acquisition counts do not capture the cost of local audits, global assessments, or context processing. Fewer actions therefore do not necessarily imply lower latency or token usage. Without complete model-call and timing statistics, it remains difficult to assess the trade-off between the accuracy gains and the actual computational cost.
Questions For Authors:
1. Could the authors report the average input and output token counts, number of model calls, and end-to-end latency in seconds per question for VideoPursuit, Action-Level Return, and direct inference? In particular, does the reduction in acquisition actions offset the cost of the additional local audits? Please specify the hardware and concurrency settings, and distinguish the cost of initial indexing and ASR preprocessing from per-question inference with cached preprocessing results.
2.For questions that depend on event order or evidence from multiple segments, do the candidate intervals provided by OpenCLIP cover the necessary evidence? The appendix shows that local retrieval can correct an early localization error and resolve temporal order, but it is unclear how consistently this recovery works. Could the authors report candidate evidence coverage, the additional acquisition actions needed after navigation errors, and the proportion of runs that exhaust the budget? Comparisons that remove the index or replace the navigation features would also help clarify how candidate navigation affects accuracy and search efficiency.

3.What fundamentally distinguishes VideoPursuit from existing retrieval-agent methods such as VideoExplorer, REVEAL, and VideoSEAL in its retrieval control and information flow? Do these differences go beyond workflow changes such as organizing multiple retrieval actions into a local loop or adjusting which context each component can access? A concrete comparison of the mechanisms, or controlled experiments, would help clarify which capabilities are introduced by this design and are not already available in existing methods.
